How to use this document

Rule 1 · apply within 48 hours

Never read a module without doing its drill within 48 hours. Reading feels like progress; only practice is progress. Aim for 1 hour learning : 2 hours doing.

Rule 2 · one variable at a time

When something isn't working (reply rate, close rate, retention), change one thing, run it for a week, measure. Changing five things at once teaches you nothing.

Rule 3 · log everything

Five lines a day: what you did, numbers, one thing that worked, one fix, energy level 1–10. After 30 days the log is better than any coach. It's also your proof.

Rule 4 · simulated ≠ real

Sales Dojo is the gym. Real calls and DMs are the match. Use the Dojo to warm up and to drill weak spots. From week 5 onward, real reps should outnumber simulated ones.

Part 00

Straight talk: what is realistic

You asked not to be told what you want to hear. Here is what the evidence says, including the parts that are inconvenient.

Five hard truths

1Content will not pay you in 90 days. Plan for that.

Ali Abdaal made essentially nothing from his first ~85 videos over about nine months, while working as a doctor.[10] Cleo Abram spent about five years at Vox learning the craft before going independent in January 2022.[1] Johnny Harris also spent about five years at Vox, with his own channel as a side project, before leaving in 2020.[5] Mark Rober worked nine years at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory and four at Apple while his channel grew.[7] The pattern is clear: the people you admire kept a paycheck while building. Your paycheck will come from sales.

2Sales can pay in 60–90 days. It can also pay zero.

Entry-level sales is one of the few skilled jobs that hire people with no degree in the subject and no experience, because your output (booked calls, revenue) is measurable within weeks. But commission-only roles are volatile, many "opportunities" are scams (Part 11), and plenty of beginners quit with nothing. The difference between people who get paid and people who don't is mostly volume of real conversations plus honest review of each one.

3Do not split 50/50 for the first 90 days.

Two half-efforts make zero income. Run roughly 65% sales / 35% content until you have income, then rebalance with data at day 90. If you land a full-time sales job, the job is the sales block and content drops to about 1 hour a day plus a weekend batch.

4Sales and content are one skill wearing two outfits.

A sales call is content for an audience of one; a video is a sales pitch to an audience of thousands. Both need a hook, a clear problem, tension, a simple explanation, proof, and a call to action. Your voice training helps your videos. Your hook writing helps your cold openers. Your sales conversations tell you what people actually care about, which becomes video ideas. This is the main reason pursuing both is rational, not greedy.

5Motivation is weather. Systems are climate.

You will have bad days, sick days, errands, family stuff. The plan includes a 30-minute Minimum Viable Day so a bad day costs you 30 minutes of progress, not the whole week. Goggins-style intensity is useful in short bursts; as a daily operating mode it leads to burnout. Part 06 shows how to use both.

The unifying idea

Attention → Trust → Clarity → Action.

Every module in this document trains one of those four. When you're unsure what to work on, ask which of the four is weakest right now, and work on that.

Two cautionary cases

BYJU'S · pressure selling

Investigations by Context (Thomson Reuters Foundation), the BBC and others reported aggressive tactics: repeated cold calls, pitches implying children would fall behind, and families unaware they were signing loans. India's child-rights body summoned the CEO. The company denied mis-selling.[20] Lesson: high-pressure closing can hit short-term targets while destroying customers, careers and the company's name. Remote closing is a real job, but the space is full of programs that charge thousands for a "certification" and promise income. Reports say the US FTC warned and later shut down one such operator, The Sales Mentor (also marketed as Sales Closer Academy).[18] Verify on ftc.gov before relying on that. Lesson: legitimate employers pay you. Part 11 has a checklist for vetting gigs.

Part 03

Fear → reps: your exposure ladder

"Everything scares me" is the most important thing you told me. The honest version: fear doesn't leave before you act. It leaves because you act, in steps small enough and often enough.

Why this works

Clinical psychology has a well-tested method for fear called graded exposure. You list feared situations from least to most scary, start near the bottom, and repeat each one until the fear drops before moving up.[28][29] Avoidance gives instant relief, and that relief teaches your brain the situation really was dangerous, so the fear stays.[28] Every rung you repeat teaches the opposite. Goggins calls it callusing the mind; therapists call it exposure. Same mechanism, different vocabulary.

The reframe

You don't need confidence to act competently. Scripts, checklists and this ladder carry you while confidence catches up. Confidence is the result of reps, not the entry ticket.

The rules

  1. Rate your fear 0–10 before each attempt (therapists call this a SUDS score) and write it in your daily log.
  2. Start on the rung that scores about 3–4. Not 0 (no growth), not 8 (you'll quit).
  3. Repeat a rung daily until it scores 2 or less, or feels boring. Then step up one rung.
  4. Stay until the fear starts to fall. Leaving at the peak teaches escape.
  5. No safety behaviours: no deleting a post after 10 minutes, no rewriting a DM 40 times, no stopping a take halfway.
  6. Expect a spike at every new rung. The spike means you picked the right rung, not the wrong career.
  7. Bad day? Repeat yesterday's rung instead of skipping. Never drop more than one rung.

Rejection, reframed with numbers

In setter work, most messages get no reply. In the illustrative math in Part 09, 900 of 1,000 messages get nothing back. That isn't 900 people judging you; it's the base rate of the job. Your only job per message is to make it good and send it. Track the rate, not the feeling.

When to get help

Nerves about calls and cameras are normal and fade with reps. If fear is also stopping basic daily things (leaving the house, sleeping, talking to people you know), a few sessions of CBT with a qualified therapist is a smart performance tool, the way a coach is. Exposure is exactly what they'd use, with expert guidance.

Culture, one line each
  • US buyers: direct, upbeat, casual, first names immediately; they like specifics and numbers.
  • UK buyers: polite, understated, sceptical of hype. Fewer exclamation marks. "Not bad" can be praise; "interesting" can mean no.

  2. Receive via Wise, Payoneer or a bank wire. Compare fees; they differ a lot. PayPal is convenient but usually the most expensive.
  3. Keep every invoice and the foreign inward remittance record (FIRA/FIRC) your bank or payment provider issues. It's your proof of export income.
  4. File an income tax return every year. At ₹4–6 lakh a year you're likely to owe little or nothing under the new tax regime, but confirm with a chartered accountant once. It's a cheap hour.
  5. Use a separate bank account for work income from day one.

Week-1 setup checklist

  • Reliable internet plus a backup (phone hotspot). A dropped call with a US lead costs you money.
  • Power backup (UPS or inverter) if your area has outages.
  • A headset with a mic, and a quiet corner from 19:00 to 23:00.
  • LinkedIn in clear English. Headline idea: "Appointment setter · US & UK hours · science explainer creator".
  • World clocks on your phone: London, New York, Los Angeles.
  • Wise or Payoneer account opened and verified (verification can take several days).
  • Scam filter: never pay to get a job, never share an OTP, and ignore WhatsApp or Telegram "task" jobs that pay you to like videos or rate products.

Part 07

Sales curriculum: from tone to close

Thirteen modules, from smallest to biggest. Each has: what it is, what to do, a drill, and the most common mistake. Sales Dojo already contains lessons from most of the books cited here. Use it to drill each module.

S1Voice and tonality

What

On a call your voice decides whether your words are believed. You'll often hear that communication is "7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language". That's a misreading of Albert Mehrabian's 1960s experiments, which looked at single words expressing feelings. Mehrabian himself says it doesn't apply to communication in general. Words matter. Tone decides whether they land.

Do
  • Three voices (Chris Voss): positive/playful as your default; the calm, slow late-night FM DJ voice with a downward inflection for key points, limits and price; assertive almost never, because it creates pushback.
  • Pace: slow down on what matters. Nervous people speed up; confident people pause.
  • Pause 1–2 seconds after asking a question and after saying a price. Let the silence work.
  • Downward inflection on statements signals certainty. Rising tone on a statement ("We help coaches?") sounds unsure.
  • Smile while talking: it's audible. Cut fillers: um, like, basically, honestly, just.
Drill

Daily: Dojo Articulation Warm-up (5 min). Record a 60-second explanation, listen at 1×, note one fix, re-record.

Mistake

Copying a "salesy" voice. Aim for you, calm and clear, not a radio announcer.

S2Language and listening

What

Tactical empathy (Voss): make the other person feel understood, and they open up and trust you.

Do
  • Mirror: repeat the last 1–3 key words as a question. "…we've tried agencies before." → "Tried agencies before?" Then wait.
  • Label: "It sounds like…", "It seems like…", "It feels like…". Name the emotion or the situation.
  • Calibrated questions (How/What): "What's the biggest challenge there?" "How would you want this to work?"
  • Accusation audit: say their objections first. "You probably think this is another sales pitch…"
  • Summarise until they say "that's right", not "you're right". "That's right" means they feel understood.
  • One question at a time. Aim to talk less than half the call.
Drill

In normal conversations, label three emotions a day. Dojo: Never Split the Difference lessons.

Mistake

Asking a question, then answering it yourself because the silence felt awkward.

S3Buyer psychology, used ethically

What

People don't buy products; they buy a change from their current state to a better one (Keenan, Gap Selling). They act when staying the same costs more than changing.

Do
  • Hormozi's value equation ($100M Offers): Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood of achieving it) ÷ (Time delay × Effort and sacrifice). To raise value, increase the top or shrink the bottom.
  • Cialdini's seven principles (Influence): reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity. Use only true versions: real testimonials, real deadlines, real expertise.
  • Loss framing: help them see the honest cost of doing nothing, in their own numbers.
Drill

Pick any product you own. Write its value equation. Which of the four terms would you improve first?

Mistake

Fake scarcity ("only 2 spots left!"). It works once, then trust is gone for good.

S4Prospecting: finding people to talk to

What

Jeb Blount's core point (Fanatical Prospecting): an empty pipeline is the real killer. Most sales failures are activity failures.

Do
  • Ideal customer profile (ICP): who exactly, what pain, where they spend time, which events trigger buying (new funding, a new hire, a launch, a complaint post).
  • The 30-day rule: prospecting you do this month pays off over the next 90 days. Never stop, even when busy.
  • Golden hours: prospect when buyers can be reached; do admin and research outside those hours.
  • Balanced prospecting: mix phone, email, DM, referrals and content. Don't rely on one channel.
  • Personalise with one specific line. Cap research at about 2 minutes per prospect.
Drill

Dojo Company X-Ray: one company in 5 minutes. Who buys, why, what's changing.

Mistake

Researching for 20 minutes, then sending a generic message anyway.

S5Openers: call, DM and email

Call

Blount's five steps: get attention (use their name), identify yourself, give the reason for the call, a "because" statement, then ask directly for the meeting. Alternative permission opener: "I know I'm calling out of the blue. Can I take 30 seconds to tell you why, and you tell me if it's worth continuing?"

DM

Context + a genuine curiosity question. No pitch in the first message. The goal of message 1 is message 2.

Email

A 2–4 word subject. Three to five sentences: trigger → problem → proof → one ask. Readable on a phone in ten seconds.

Mistake

"Hope you're doing well! I wanted to reach out to…" Every word before the point costs you attention.

S6Qualifying: who is worth your time

B2C / high-ticket

Need (a real problem) · Urgency (why now?) · Ability (can they invest without harming themselves?) · Authority (do they decide, or is a spouse or parent involved?) · Coachability (will they actually do the work?).

B2B

Start with BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timing). Graduate to MEDDIC: Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion.

Mindset

Disqualifying is a skill, not a failure. A clear "this isn't for you" protects your time and the buyer's money, and it makes you more credible with the ones who are a fit.

S7Discovery: where deals are actually won

SPIN

From Neil Rackham's research on thousands of sales calls: Situation questions (use sparingly, they bore buyers) → Problem → Implication (what does this problem cause?) → Need-payoff (what would solving it be worth?). Implication and need-payoff questions do the heavy lifting in larger sales.

Gap

Current state (facts, pain, root cause, impact in numbers) → future state (what they want) → the gap between them is the value. The bigger and clearer the gap, the easier the close.

Do

Spend most of the call here. Get the root cause ("Why do you think that keeps happening?") and quantify it ("Roughly what does that cost you a month?"). Question bank in Part 10.

Mistake

Pitching the moment you hear a problem. Hold your solution until you understand the whole gap.

S8Presenting the offer

Do
  • Recap in their words first: "You said X, Y and Z. Did I get that right?"
  • Present only the parts that close their gap. Keenan: demo only what closes the gap.
  • Benefits beat features: connect each part to a need they stated (Rackham).
  • Teach something (from The Challenger Sale): an insight about their problem they didn't have. Your sci-tech research habit is an edge here.
  • Say the price plainly, then stop talking.
Mistake

Feature dumping: listing everything the product does "just in case".

S9Objections

Sequence

Pause → Label → Clarify/isolate → Respond → Confirm. "It sounds like price is the concern. Aside from price, is this the right solution for you?" → respond with options, evidence or a reframe → "Does that address it?"

Prevent

Most objections are discovery failures (Rackham). If you hear "too expensive", the gap wasn't big or clear enough. Fix discovery, not your rebuttal.

Drill

Dojo Objection Blitz daily in weeks 3–4. Full response table in Part 10.

Mistake

Arguing. You can win the argument and lose the sale. Respond with questions.

S10Closing

CLOSER

Hormozi's sequence:[14] Clarify why they're here → Label the problem → Overview past pain (what they've tried and why it failed) → Sell the vacation (the outcome, not the flight) → Explain away concerns → Reinforce the decision after they buy.

The ask

Direct, simple, then silence. "Based on what you've told me, X, Y and Z, I think this is a fit. Want to get started?" Then say nothing until they answer.

Advance

Rackham: every call ends with an advance (a concrete next step with a date), not a continuation ("let's stay in touch").

Mistake

Never actually asking. Many beginners present beautifully, then wait for the buyer to volunteer.

S11Follow-up and a simple CRM

Do
  • Many deals aren't decided on the first call. Use a follow-up cadence (Part 10): recap → value → check-in → polite close-the-file.
  • A CRM can be a Google Sheet. Columns: name · source · stage · last touch · next step + date · notes.
  • Rule: no lead without a next date.

S12Metrics and self-coaching

Setter

Touches → replies → conversations → qualified → booked → showed. Watch the reply rate and the show rate.

Closer

Show rate · close rate · cash collected per call · refund rate. A high close rate with high refunds means you're overselling.

B2B

Activities → meetings → opportunities → won. Pipeline coverage: pipeline value relative to quota.

Review

Score one call a day (Part 10). Record only with consent, and follow local laws on call recording. Weekly: find the leakiest stage and fix one thing.

S13Ethics and reputation

Rules
  • Never lie about results, scarcity or what's included.
  • Don't sell to people who can't afford it or won't benefit. Never push credit or loans on someone who's stretched (the BYJU'S lesson).
  • The test: would you sell this, at this price, to your own family member in this situation?
Why

Your reputation compounds just like content. Honest closers get referrals, testimonials and better jobs. Pushy ones get refunds, chargebacks and bad reviews.

Part 08

Content curriculum: from idea to audience

Twelve modules for science and tech explainers. The goal for the first 90 days is skill and portfolio, not follower count.

C1Define your show (positioning)

What

Cleo Abram's growth is attributed to a specific show with a defined brand (optimism about technology) and an idea checklist: is it genuinely visual, is there a good explanation, is the story optimistic?[3]

Do
  • One line: "[Topic] for [audience] through [lens]." Examples: "The physics hiding in everyday things." "New tech explained in 60 seconds, with one honest caveat." "How the machines around you actually work."
  • Write your own 3-question idea filter. Every idea must pass all three.
  • Language: English (decided). A bigger, more competitive market, so the show's lens and quality must differentiate you. Bonus: every English video is proof of communication for US/UK employers.
Mistake

"Random interesting stuff." Nobody subscribes to random.

C2Idea sourcing and research

Sources

University press releases (EurekAlert!), Nature and Science news, Quanta Magazine, company engineering blogs, product launches, Our World in Data, questions on Reddit and in your comments, and outlier videos in your niche (videos doing 3× or more their channel's average).

Do
  • Keep an idea bank of 100 or more. Score each 1–5 on curiosity, how visual it is, and your ability to explain it.
  • Accuracy rule: two credible sources per claim. Label speculation clearly. "Huge if true" is itself a caveat format.
  • If you get something wrong, correct it publicly. In science content, trust is the product.

C3Packaging: title and thumbnail first

What

MrBeast's leaked production guide (2024) reportedly has the team start with the title and thumbnail, and track three core metrics: click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed.[21]

Do
  • Write 10 titles for each long video. Pick the clearest promise, not the cleverest phrase.
  • Thumbnail: one subject, at most 1–3 words, high contrast, readable at phone size.
  • For shorts, the first frame and on-screen text are the packaging.
  • The video must deliver what the title promised, fast. Misleading packaging kills retention.

C4Hooks

Window

The first 1–3 seconds for shorts. The first 30–60 seconds for long-form, which the MrBeast guide reportedly calls the most important minute because that's when most viewers leave.[21]

Types
  • Misconception: "You were taught X. It's wrong." This works as a hook and as teaching (Derek Muller's research).[9]
  • Stakes: "This could make every battery on Earth obsolete."
  • Visual surprise: show the weird thing first, explain after.
  • Specific number: "This chip does 1,000 trillion operations a second."
  • Tension question: "Why don't planes fly in a straight line?"
Drill

Write 10 hooks a day for two weeks (5 minutes). Dojo Ad & Copy Lab trains the same skill.

C5Story structure

ABT

Scientist-turned-filmmaker Randy Olson's template: "And… But… Therefore…". Setup, then problem, then consequence. The South Park creators make the same point: scenes should connect with "but" and "therefore", never "and then".

Do
  • Open a loop early, close it late. Ask the question at second 3, answer it at second 50.
  • Include people: a scientist, a team, a failure. Stories are about someone.
  • One analogy per concept, and check that it doesn't teach something false.
  • End with "so what": what it means for the viewer, plus the honest caveat.

C6Scriptwriting for the ear

Steps

Research notes → one-sentence thesis → beat outline (ABT) → draft → read aloud → cut 20–30% → add a visuals column (a two-column script: words | what's on screen).

Feynman

Explain the topic to an imaginary 12-year-old. Wherever you stumble, you don't understand it yet. Go back and research.

Style

Short sentences. One idea per sentence. Concrete beats abstract. Write how you talk.

C7On-camera delivery

Do
  • Everything in S1 (voice) applies: one skill, two uses.
  • Energy about 20% above normal conversation, because the camera flattens you. Look into the lens.
  • Record in short chunks. Do the first 3 seconds five times and keep the best.
  • Camera-shy? Follow the camera ladder in Part 03: faceless first, hands next, face by about week 11. A face tends to build trust faster.

C8Minimum viable production kit

Kit

Your phone's rear camera · a clip-on mic (audio matters more than video) · window light · a cheap tripod. Editing: CapCut to start, DaVinci Resolve (free) later. Visuals: your own footage, screen recordings, simple diagrams in Canva or Keynote, free stock footage.

Rule

No new gear until 30 videos are posted. Gear is the most comfortable form of procrastination.

C9Editing for retention

Do

Cut every pause and breath. In shorts, change the visual every few seconds. Use captions (many people watch muted). On-screen text reinforces the key number. Keep sound design subtle. Before exporting, watch once at full speed and note anywhere you'd swipe away, then fix it.

C10Platforms and cadence

Days 1–90

YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, plus TikTok where available: 3 a week, the same video cross-posted. These are fast feedback reps. One long video (5–10 min) every 2–3 weeks, built from your best short.

Bridge

From week 8, post text versions on LinkedIn or X twice a week. LinkedIn is where Justin Welsh built his business, and where tech sales hiring managers spend time.[12]

Later

A newsletter (an audience you own, not rented from an algorithm) from around month 6.

C11Analytics and iteration

Metrics
  • Long-form: click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed, and the retention curve (where exactly people leave).
  • Shorts: viewed vs. swiped away, average percentage viewed, shares and saves (strongest signal that a video was valuable), comments.
Method

Change one variable per experiment: hook type, length, topic, thumbnail style. Monthly: compare your top 3 and bottom 3 and write down why you think they differ.

Mistake

Watching subscriber count daily. It's a lagging number. Retention and shares are leading numbers.

C12Monetisation ladder (realistic order)

Order
  1. Indirect: content makes you a stronger sales hire or wins you clients. This comes first and is usually the biggest early.
  2. Services: editing or scripting for others (Option C).
  3. Sponsorships: need a consistent, engaged audience in a clear niche.
  4. Ad revenue: needs YouTube Partner Program eligibility. Third-party guides describe a lower tier around 500 subscribers (fan funding) and full ad revenue at 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 public watch hours in 12 months, or a Shorts-views route.[22] Check the official YouTube Help page; rules change.
  5. Own products: courses, newsletters, communities. Highest margin, needs the most trust (the Welsh and Abdaal models).
The 2-hour content sprint (one deep block)
  1. 0:00–0:20 · pick an idea from the bank, read two sources, write a one-sentence thesis.
  2. 0:20–0:45 · write five hooks, pick one, outline with ABT.
  3. 0:45–1:05 · script in two columns (words | visuals), read aloud, cut 25%.
  4. 1:05–1:30 · film: hook ×5 takes, then the body in chunks.
  5. 1:30–2:00 · rough edit plus captions. Polish and post in shallow time.

Publish the B+ version. Your first 30 videos are tuition. Perfectionism at this stage is fear in disguise. Ship it, then fix one thing in the next video.

The insight

Getting 20% better at four stages roughly doubles the output (1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 ≈ 2.07), with no extra effort at the top of the funnel. That's why the weekly "find the leak, fix one thing" habit beats simply working more hours.

Show-rate checklist (setters)

  • Book the call while they're still talking to you. Offer two specific times; don't just send a link and hope.
  • Confirm immediately with the time in their time zone.
  • Send a short value piece before the call (a 2-minute video or a case study) so they arrive warmer.
  • Remind them 24 hours and 1 hour before, by personal message, not only automated email.
  • If they no-show, follow up within 10 minutes, kindly. Rebook once.

Traps, scams & red flags

Most beginners don't fail from lack of talent. They fail from one of these.

Vetting a setter or closer gig: ask before you accept

AskGreen flagRed flag
Do I pay anything to join?No. They pay you.A "certification" or "training fee" before any work
Where do leads come from?Their content, ads or an existing list; numbers shared"You generate your own leads", for a role called closer
What's the offer, the price and the refund rate?Clear answers; a product that gets real resultsVague; income claims; "just sell the dream"

The ten traps

  1. Pay-to-get-hired academies promising income. Legitimate employers pay you.[18]
  2. Selling something that hurts buyers. Short-term money, long-term damage (BYJU'S).[20]
  3. Tutorial hell. Courses and books feel productive. Keep the 1:2 learning-to-doing ratio.
  4. Gear before reps. No new equipment before 30 videos.
  5. Vanity metrics. Followers and likes. Track retention, replies, show rate and cash.
  1. Comparing your chapter 1 to someone's chapter 20. Cleo Abram's 2.5-year sprint came after five years at Vox.
  2. Hustle culture and sleep debt. It wrecks the consolidation that makes skill permanent.
  3. Niche-hopping every two weeks. Commit for 90 days, then review with data.
  4. Doing everything at 100%. That's why the MVD exists. Sustainable beats heroic.
  5. Waiting until you feel ready. Readiness comes after reps, not before.
The one-line test

If an "opportunity" talks more about lifestyle (cars, laptops on beaches, "quit your 9–5") than about the product, the customer and the work, walk away.
